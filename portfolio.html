Method

Every session, the same five moments.

Predictability is the teaching, not a limit on it: a fixed structure and a visual agenda mean pupils spend their attention on the language, not on guessing what comes next. Every answer counts, by voice, sign, gesture, pictogram or communicator.

One unit is a step on a staircase

Twelve units over two years, anchored to the CEFR (Pre-A1 to A1, 2020 descriptors). Nothing is taught once and left behind: each unit reactivates the language of the previous ones.

Units 1–4

Pre-A1

Level 2 · Construction

Units 5–8

Early A1

100–140 words, 30–50 productive. I like / don't like · Do you like…? · Can I have…? Preferences with sentence frames, guided role-play.

Units 9–12

A1

160–200+ words, 80–100 productive. I can/can't · I'm wearing · Wh- questions. Semi-spontaneous production, short own descriptions.

Adaptation

Same target, different doors in.

All my teaching has been in special education. For most of my pupils English is a second language; for some, from English-speaking homes, it is their first. So every lesson is planned along three axes, child by child:

🌍Language history

What place English has in the child's life: first language, second language, or first contact. It changes the input, never the expectation.

💬Communication channel

Voice, sign, gesture, pictogram or communicator. The channel is the child's choice; the language act is the same.

🧩Support needs

What each profile needs to reach the same content: pre-teaching, previewing, more time, a different position in the room.

The same lesson, three learners

A worked projection onto a Key Stage 1 class: how the five moments of one session flex for three different profiles. The adaptations are the ones I use daily; the class is illustrative.

What never changes: the target language, the cognitive demand, the song and the expectation. Adapting means opening more doors into the same room, not building a smaller room.

Assessment

Progress, in steps small enough to see.

For every strand I record comprehension and production separately, each by any valid channel. A level scale alone misses most of what happens here: the real movement lives between the levels, and that movement is what gets written down.

What a record looks like · U1, “Presentation”

SecureProduces “I'm [name]!” spontaneously, in context
ExpectedCompletes the frame with their name when asked
EmergingSelects their name on the communicator
SupportedPoints to, or signs, “name” with help

Between any two descriptors, a dozen recordable steps: full prompt, partial prompt, peer model, unprompted once, unprompted usually. Observed inside the activity, never in a test the child can feel.

The records read onto any framework

  • Cambridge Primary ESL (0057) · the staircase maps onto its strands and stages
  • Bell Foundation EAL framework · comprehension and production by skill reads onto its A–E bands
  • Assess · Plan · Do · Review · every record feeds the next planning decision
  • The Engagement Model · for learners below subject-specific study, the small steps are the evidence

Research

The question underneath all of it.

Most education systems quietly exempt children with special educational needs from learning an additional language, often with no evidence behind the exemption. I am mapping that field in a pre-registered scoping review: how these children learn additional languages, across the whole spectrum of need and in several languages of publication. The protocol is public on the Open Science Framework, registered before any formal search was run.
